The AI writes. The server knows. Code enforces.

That's the rule I'm building around, and it settled most design arguments early.

When someone says "run my lead generation ads", three different jobs have to happen:

1. Writing: the copy, the headlines, the angles.
Your AI already does this well. Claude, ChatGPT, whichever you use. So the tool doesn't add its own AI model. That would mean one more bill, one more key, and me choosing an AI vendor for every customer.

2. Knowing: what actually works on Meta right now. Which objective fits which goal, how to structure a test, what a budget can learn from.
This lives in the tool as playbooks, and the AI is told to read them before it touches an ad account, without the user having to ask.

3. Enforcing: spend limits, approvals, what's allowed.
This is code. Never a prompt.

The third one is the line I won't cross. A playbook is advice to the AI. If the playbook said "go ahead and spend", nothing would happen, because the spend limit and the approval step are checked in code whatever the AI decides.

If you're building with AI agents in marketing, I'd suggest the same split: let the model do language, let your system hold the knowledge, and keep anything involving money or publishing in deterministic code.
